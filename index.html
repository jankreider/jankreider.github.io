Research

The evolutionary transitions from unicellular to multicellular life and from solitary to eusocial insects have fundamentally reshaped the history of life on Earth. I want to understand why these transitions occurred in some organisms but not in others. My work focuses on how phenotypic plasticity in unicellular and solitary organisms enabled the evolution of division of labour and biological complexity.

Current project · Lund

The transition to multicellularity

unicellular → facultatively multicellular → multicellular?

Green algae have transitioned to multicellularity numerous times and include unicellular, facultatively multicellular and obligately multicellular species. Have some unicellular green algae been predisposed to evolve obligate multicellularity? I investigate whether multicellularity emerges more often in response to genetic mutation in some species than in others. With comparative transcriptomics and genomics, I study how gene co-expression and transcription factors differ between green algae with different degrees of multicellularity. In models of evolving gene regulatory networks, I ask whether plasticity in multicellular group formation favours obligate multicellularity.

The transition to eusociality

solitary → facultatively social → eusocial?

Bees, ants, wasps and termites live in colonies, usually with one reproductive queen (and in termites, a king) supported by hundreds or thousands of sterile workers. How does eusociality evolve from solitary ancestors? Using behavioural experiments, I showed that low temperature triggers Exoneura robusta bees to share nests rather than nest alone. In individual-based simulations, I demonstrated that resource sharing is sufficient for division of labour to emerge among identical individuals, and that maternal manipulation of offspring body size can favour eusociality with larger queens and smaller workers.
